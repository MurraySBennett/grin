\begin{minipage}[t]{\colw}
\qhead{the problem}{What are we actually trying to measure?}

{\bodyfont
Two trainees can score \emph{identically} on a diagnostic task and be wrong in
completely different ways. One cannot see the feature. One sees it but has
drifted their criterion. One sees both features but cannot pull them apart.

General Recognition Theory separates these. It gives each person a perceptual
space: where the stimuli sit, how noisy each is, whether the two features are
perceived independently, and where the decision bounds fall.
\par}

\begin{callout}
\textbf{Why it matters clinically.} A trainee who confuses an irregular
\emph{border} with variegated \emph{colour} needs different practice from one
who simply cannot see colour variation at all. Accuracy alone cannot tell them
apart. The representation can.
\end{callout}

{\bodyfont
A $4\times4$ identification matrix carries exactly 12 independent numbers, so
we work in the 12-parameter identified coordinates. Saturated, therefore
identified --- no restricted-model hierarchy needed.
\par}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------- column 2
\begin{minipage}[t]{\colw}
\qhead{recovery}{Does it recover the truth?}

\gfig[0.34]{poster_recovery.png}{1.0}{Predicted vs.\ true on held-out simulations.
Sensitivities are recovered tightly; correlations are not --- and that is a
property of the data, not the method. Right: the two coordinates of each stimulus recovered jointly.}

{\bodyfont
Sensitivities come back at \chk{$r \approx 0.98$}. Perceptual correlations are
much noisier, \chk{$r \approx 0.75$}. Maximum likelihood shows the \emph{same}
split on the same matrices: weak correlations are genuinely unrecoverable from
a single confusion matrix.
\par}

\gfig[0.40]{calibration.png}{1.0}{Simulation-based calibration: flat rank histogram,
coverage on the diagonal.}

{\bodyfont
The posterior is not just narrow --- it is \textbf{honest}. That is what makes
it safe to act on mid-experiment.
\par}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------- column 3
\begin{minipage}[t]{\colw}
\qhead{speed}{Is it fast enough to matter?}

\gfig[0.44]{accuracy_crossover.png}{1.0}{Against the MLE workflow people actually run:
fit every model class, keep the AIC/BIC winner.}

\bignum{\chk{$\sim10^{4}\times$}}{faster than the realistic MLE workflow}

{\bodyfont
Fast enough is not a bonus here --- it is the whole enabling condition. At
\chk{$\sim$3\,\textmu s} a posterior update fits inside an inter-stimulus
interval, so the model can sit \emph{inside} the trial loop instead of waiting
until the session is over.

And below \chk{$\sim$100} trials per stimulus the network is also \emph{more
accurate} than MLE: the prior regularises exactly where likelihood
optimisation diverges. It also never fails to return an answer, which the R
implementations sometimes do.
\par}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------- column 4
\begin{minipage}[t]{\colw}
\qhead{limits}{Where does it break?}

\gfig[0.42]{constructs.png}{1.0}{Construct probabilities are calibrated; the
right-hand panel is the identifiability frontier for perceptual independence.}

{\bodyfont
Separability is recovered sharply (\chk{$\approx0.94$}) and matches exact
AIC/BIC selection. Perceptual independence does not: accuracy climbs with the
size of the true correlation.
\par}

\begin{callout}
\textbf{We are not overselling PI.} Near-independence is information-limited,
so we report a calibrated \emph{probability} rather than forcing a verdict.
Decisional separability stays assumed --- response times do not rescue it, and
recovering it needs bound manipulations we have not run.
\end{callout}

{\bodyfont
Adding response times helps where you would expect and not where you would
hope: it buys processing architecture (\chk{$0.88$}) and dimension neglect
(\chk{$0.97$}), but moves PI only \chk{$0.60 \to 0.65$}.
\par}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------- column 5
\begin{minipage}[t]{\colw}
\qhead{the payoff}{What does that buy you?}

\bignum{\chk{55\%}}{of trials saved by stopping each participant at a precision
threshold instead of a fixed budget}

\bignum{\chk{46\%}}{fewer trials for a simulated learner to reach an expert
target under a representation-tracking curriculum}

{\bodyfont
\textbf{An honest negative:} information-gain stimulus selection roughly
\emph{ties} round-robin. With four symmetric stimuli, balanced coverage is
already near-optimal. The win comes from knowing \emph{when to stop} and
\emph{what to teach}, not from cleverer sampling.
\par}

\begin{callout}
\textbf{Next.} Take this to melanoma screening, where border and colour genuinely
overlap; relax decisional separability; and move from selecting stimuli in a
pool to generating them. The targeted-training result rests on an
\emph{assumed} learning dynamic --- it demonstrates the mechanism, not a
validated protocol.
\end{callout}
\end{minipage}
