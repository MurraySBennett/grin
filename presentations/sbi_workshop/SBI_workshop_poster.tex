% \qhead{The problem}{What we are measuring}
\qhead{}{What we are measuring}

{\bodyfont
General recognition theory (GRT) describes how a person perceives and categorises a stimulus made of two features.
Stimuli are placed in a perceptual space with the goal of recovering (i) their location, (ii) their noise, (iii) perceptual independence, and where the person sets their decision bounds.\par\medskip\medskip}

\begin{callout}
\textbf{In the clinic.} For melanoma identification, confusing an irregular \emph{border} with variegated \emph{colour} is a different failure from not seeing the colour variegation at all and each needs different practice. Accuracy alone cannot tell them apart but a multidimensional representation can.\medskip
\end{callout}

{\bodyfont
The same performance split can show up in training. Three trainees could score identically yet differ underneath: one lacks the senstivity to see a feature, another perceives it but has drifted their criterion, and a third perceives both but cannot separate them. Critically, each requires different training.
\par\medskip\medskip}

{\bodyfont
We aim to recover the representation behind multidimensional categorisation accuracy to facilitate efficient perceptual training.\par}


\end{minipage}%

\begin{minipage}[t]{\colw}
% \qhead{Recovery}{Recovery, and its limit}
\qhead{}{Recovery, and its limit}

\gfig[2]{poster_recovery.png}{\textbf{Top:} estimated vs.\ true on held-out
simulations --- sensitivities tightly, correlations not, a property of the data
not the method; the third panel is each stimulus's joint error. \textbf{Bottom:}
interval coverage sits on the diagonal.}

{\bodyfont
Recovery is uneven. Sensitivities return at $r \approx 0.975$, correlations far noisier at
$r \approx 0.576$, but maximum likelihood shows the \emph{same} split.%
\par\medskip\medskip}

{\bodyfont
Weak correlations are genuinely unrecoverable from one confusion matrix, but the posterior permits \textbf{honest} credible interval coverage at their stated rate, making the method safe to act on mid-experiment.
\par}

\end{minipage}%
\hfill
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------- column 3
\begin{minipage}[t]{\colw}
% \qhead{Speed}{Instant estimation}
\qhead{}{Instant estimation}

\gfig[3]{speed_accuracy.png}{Speed x accuracy trade-off compared against our own MLE and two leading R packages (\texttt{mdsdt, grtools}).
\textbf{Top:}
fast is left, accurate is up --- GRIN is both fast and accurate.
Faster than all other tools and no less accurate than the R packages or Python MLE.
\textbf{Bottom:} accuracy rises with sample size, and GRIN leads when data are scarce.
\par\medskip\medskip\medskip\medskip}

\bignum{100x}{faster than the realistic MLE workflow.}

{\bodyfont\medskip
This speed wholly represents the enabling condition.
At $\sim$1'\,ms a posterior update fits inside an inter-stimulus interval, so the model can sit \emph{inside} the trial loop and never fail to return an answer (which R and MLE fitters sometimes do).
\par}
\end{minipage}%

\gfig[2]{constructs.png}{}
\begin{callout}
\textbf{Invisible independence.} Every method drifts toward signalling independence as the real
correlation vanishes. Weak correlations barely affect a confusion matrix, so a challenging to identify.
GRIN can help here by reporting the uncertainty where  the others are committed to a point estimate.
\end{callout}

{\bodyfont
Separability is recovered sharply but correlation structure is not. This is true for all methods, not just ours.\par}
