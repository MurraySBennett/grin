An ordinary 2×2 identification task. A stimulus appears briefly, you name which of the four it was, and the panel on the right refits the GRT model after every response. Nothing is simulated except the stimulus — the responses are yours, and the model is the same network the R and Python packages ship.

That refitting is the point. Fitting a GRT model by numerical optimisation takes seconds, so it can only happen once a session is over. Here it takes a fraction of a millisecond, so the design can respond to it while the participant is still working.

Errors come from the presentation time and the deadline, not from making the stimuli nearly identical. A speeded task is how an identification experiment normally lands in the informative accuracy range; shorten the presentation or tighten the deadline to push accuracy down.

These are the four stimuli. The response keys are arranged to match: the key's row codes the first dimension and its column the second, so E/I are the high options and F/J the low ones.

What you are looking at

The ellipses are the perceptual distributions the model infers for each of the four stimuli; the dashed lines are the decision bounds. Early on they move a lot, because a handful of trials cannot pin down twelve parameters. As trials accumulate they settle and the posterior-width trace falls. When it crosses your target, the task stops.

That is the stopping rule the packages expose as grin_stop_on_precision() and gt.stop_on_precision(), evaluated between trials rather than after the session. The adaptive stopping tutorial shows how to wire it into your own experiment.

Two things this demo does deliberately. Trials on which you miss the deadline are discarded, not scored as errors — a missed deadline is not a confusion, and counting it as one would bias every parameter. And the intervals shown are the network's own: the paper reports that its correlation intervals run narrower than nominal, which the Evidence page shows directly.
