This is an ordinary 2×2 identification task: a rectangle appears, varying in width (dimension A) and height (dimension B), and you say which of the four it was. Nothing is simulated except the stimulus — the responses are yours, and the panel on the right is the same network the R and Python packages ship, refitted after every trial.

That refitting is the point. Fitting a GRT model by numerical optimisation takes seconds, so it can only happen once the session is over. Here it takes a fraction of a millisecond, so the design can react to it while you are still working.

Noise controls how hard the task is. Turn it down and you will sit near ceiling, where the paper shows the within-stimulus correlations are least identifiable; turn it up and the sensitivities become hard to pin down. The accuracy readout tells you which side you are on.

What you are looking at

The ellipses are the perceptual distributions the model infers for each of the four stimuli, and the dashed lines are the decision bounds. Early on they are all over the place, because four trials cannot pin down twelve parameters. As trials accumulate they settle, and the posterior-width trace below them falls.

When that trace crosses your target the task stops. That is the stopping rule the packages expose as grin_stop_on_precision() and gt.stop_on_precision(), evaluated between trials rather than after the session — see the recipes for how to wire it into your own experiment.

One honest caveat: the intervals shown here are the network's own, and the paper reports that its correlation intervals run narrower than nominal. The Evidence page shows this directly, and the packages ship an optional correction for it.
