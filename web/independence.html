The imprecision in identifying independence

Designing a study given the limited information in a confusion matrix

It is difficult to tell whether two features are perceptually independent (when the within-stimulus correlation is zero). This page uses the same tools as the rest of the site to show why, and what it means for designing a study. Our results suggest the difficulty comes from the data and the task, not from any particular fitting method. Evidence covers how well the rest of the model is recovered.

2. How often GRIN detects a correlation

Simulate many participants with true correlations between −0.9 and 0.9, fit each with GRIN, and record how often it reports a correlation. Each dimension has a one in two chance of not being separable, as in GRIN's training. Sensitivity ranges from d′ 1.2 to 4.0.

GRIN was trained on up to 1,000 trials per stimulus, but its accuracy was only checked up to 500. Treat results above 500 as a look beyond the tested range.

The recovery frontier for independence

How often GRIN reports a correlation, against the size of the true correlation, |ρ|. A correlation of −0.5 and one of 0.5 fall at the same point. Near zero the curve should stay low, which means GRIN correctly calls the dimensions independent. It should rise as the correlation grows. The stretch in between, where real but weak correlations are missed, is the recovery frontier for independence.

3. Response times add a little information about the correlation

You might expect response times to fill the gap. When the tilt changes, accuracy and response time change together, so timing adds little beyond what accuracy already shows. In 1,800 simulated participants, the response-time network chose the correct correlation class about 55% of the time, against about 46% for the network that uses only the table of answers. Response times are far more useful for identifying how the features are processed (the processing architecture), which the response-time network got right about 85% of the time, where chance is 20%.

4. What this suggests

Estimating the correlation from a single table of answers is hard, and the difficulty seems to lie in the data and the task rather than in any one method. We would expect other methods that use the same data to struggle in the same way.

Given that, a few directions seem reasonable to us. Treat the correlation as a distribution over the options rather than a single winner, and keep it separate from the separability decision, which is identified far more firmly. An unresolved correlation is best read as unresolved, not as independence and not as a failed fit.

Where correlation genuinely matters, the leverage seems to lie in the data you collect more than the method you fit. Asking participants for confidence ratings, or having them respond to the two dimensions separately or in sequence rather than as one joint response, could add information a single joint matrix does not carry. These are potential directions, and we would welcome tests of them.

Finally, methods designed to bring in extra information are worth reaching for where they apply. Approaches that pool across participants, such as GRT-wIND (Soto et al., 2015), can recover group-level structure that a single participant's matrix cannot, and where that kind of design or data is available it is the better tool for the job. None of this counts against maximum likelihood or the existing GRT software. On the parts of the model that are identified, maximum likelihood, the existing GRT software and GRIN give similar estimates.
