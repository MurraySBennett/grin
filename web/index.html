GRIN

A free tool for analysing identification experiments in perception research. It estimates the same quantities as the standard method, in milliseconds per participant, and gives a range of likely values for each one.

What this is

Some things we see differ in two ways at once. A face, for example, can be happy or sad, and male or female. General Recognition Theory (GRT) asks whether the mind keeps those two features apart, or whether one affects how we see the other. In a GRT study, people see one item at a time and name both features. From the pattern of mistakes, GRT estimates how the mind represents the two features.

The standard way to fit GRT (maximum likelihood) searches for the best-fitting answer separately for every participant and every model class, and returns one best guess. GRIN replaces that search with a neural network trained in advance on simulated experiments. Given new data, it answers in milliseconds. For each estimate it gives a range of likely values, and for each main conclusion it gives a probability.

Everything on this site runs in your browser. Your data stay on your computer. Nothing is uploaded.

What GRIN does

What GRIN is good at

  • Speed. Each participant requires only a quick pass rather than an entirely new search, so large studies and analyses within an experiment become practical.
  • Uncertainty range. For each estimate GRIN gives a range of likely values, and for each main question a probability. We tested how often these ranges contain the true value in simulated data it was not trained on. The ranges for correlations are too narrow. See Evidence.
  • Small samples. It returns an estimate even when the standard method fails to find one. That does not make a small sample more informative. With few trials, expect wide ranges and uncertain conclusions. GRIN was tested on 5 to 500 trials per stimulus.

What it is not

  • Importantly, GRIN only knows what it was trained on. It was trained on 1.2 million simulated 2×2 identification experiments, with 1 to 1,000 trials per stimulus and correlations between −0.9 and 0.9. It checks your file and warns when your data fall outside the range it was trained or tested on. It cannot tell whether GRT is the right model for your data, or catch every case where your data fall outside what it has seen.
  • It handles 2×2 designs only (two features, two levels each), and it assumes each decision line is fixed (decisional separability, see Learn).
  • It does not interpret results for you. A probability near 0.5, or weak support for the best-fitting model class, means the data do not settle the question.
