GRIN

General Recognition Inversion Network, fit GRT models to identification data in milliseconds, with uncertainty you can trust.

What this is

General Recognition Theory asks a precise question: when someone identifies a stimulus that varies on two dimensions, are those dimensions processed independently? GRT answers it by treating each stimulus as a bivariate distribution in a perceptual space, and reading the shape of that space out of a confusion matrix.

Maximum likelihood approaches this by running an optimiser for each participant and each candidate model, and returns a point estimate. GRIN replaces the optimiser with a neural network trained on millions of simulated experiments. Inference becomes a single forward pass, about a millisecond, and returns a posterior over parameter estimates and model classifications, not just a point.

Everything on this site runs in your browser. Your data are never uploaded anywhere.

What it does and doesn't do

What GRIN is good at

  • Speed. A participant is a forward pass. Fitting 200 people is about as fast as fitting one, which makes hierarchical and adaptive designs practical to run.
  • Calibrated uncertainty. The network returns a posterior over each parameter and model class. We check that its 90% intervals actually contain the truth 90% of the time, see Validate.
  • Small samples. It was trained down to a single trial per stimulus, so it degrades gracefully instead of failing to converge.

What it is not

  • It is not assumption-free. GRIN is amortised over a prior. Feed it something that prior never generated and it will hand you a confident answer that means nothing. The app checks for this and says so.
  • It assumes decisional separability throughout, and (currently) 2×2 designs only.
  • It is not a replacement for thinking. A winning model class with a probability of 0.4 is not a conclusive finding, and GRIN will tell you that, too.
