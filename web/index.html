GRIN

A fast, free tool for analysing a classic kind of perception experiment. It gives the same kind of answer as the standard method in about a millisecond, and says how sure it is.

What this is

Some things we see differ in two ways at once. A face, for example, can be happy or sad, and male or female. General Recognition Theory (GRT) asks whether the mind keeps those two features apart, or whether one affects how we see the other. In a GRT study, people see one item at a time and name both features. GRT works out how the features are represented from the pattern of their mistakes.

The standard way to fit GRT (maximum likelihood) searches for the best-fitting answer separately for every participant and every version of the model, and returns one best guess. GRIN replaces that search with a neural network trained in advance on simulated experiments. Given new data, it answers in about a millisecond. For each estimate it gives a range of likely values, and for each main conclusion it gives a probability.

Everything on this site runs in your browser. Your experimental data are not uploaded for analysis.

What it does and doesn't do

What GRIN is good at

  • Speed. Each participant is a forward pass, so large batches are practical without running a fresh optimiser for every person and candidate model.
  • Tested uncertainty. The network returns an approximate posterior for each parameter and separate probabilities for the structural questions. We check interval coverage on held-out simulations; see Evidence.
  • Small samples. It can return an estimate where an optimiser may fail to converge. That does not create information: with few trials, expect wide intervals and weak structural conclusions.

What it is not

  • It makes assumptions. GRIN only knows the range of experiments it was trained on. It checks your file and warns about some data that look unlike its training examples. It cannot tell whether GRT is the right model for your data, or catch every case where your data fall outside what it has seen.
  • It assumes decisional separability throughout, and (currently) 2×2 designs only.
  • It does not interpret results for you. A probability near 0.5, or weak support for the best-fitting class of model, means the data do not settle the question.
