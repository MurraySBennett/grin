GRIN

General Recognition Inference Network: fit GRT models to identification data in milliseconds, with uncertainty that has been tested.

What this is

General Recognition Theory asks a precise question: when someone identifies a stimulus that varies on two dimensions, are those dimensions processed independently? GRT answers it by treating each stimulus as a bivariate distribution in a perceptual space, and reading the shape of that space out of a confusion matrix.

Maximum likelihood approaches this by running an optimiser for each participant and each candidate model, and returns a point estimate. GRIN replaces the optimiser with a neural network trained on simulated experiments. Inference becomes a single forward pass, about a millisecond, and returns a posterior over parameter estimates and model classifications, not just a point.

Everything on this site runs in your browser. Your experimental data are not uploaded for analysis.

What it does and doesn't do

What GRIN is good at

  • Speed. Each participant is a forward pass, so large batches are practical without running a fresh optimiser for every person and candidate model.
  • Tested uncertainty. The network returns an approximate posterior for each parameter and separate probabilities for the structural questions. We check interval coverage on held-out simulations; see Evidence.
  • Small samples. It can return an estimate where an optimiser may fail to converge. That does not create information: with few trials, expect wide intervals and weak structural conclusions.

What it is not

  • It is not assumption-free. GRIN is amortised over a fixed simulation prior. The app catches malformed inputs and some obvious training-envelope problems; it cannot prove that GRT generated your data or detect every kind of extrapolation.
  • It assumes decisional separability throughout, and (currently) 2×2 designs only.
  • It is not a replacement for thinking. A construct probability near 0.5 or weak factorized class support is not a conclusive finding.
